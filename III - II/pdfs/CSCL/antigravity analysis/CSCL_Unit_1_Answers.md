## Q1. Define Cyber Security. Explain its importance and the various security domains.

### Introduction

Cyber security is the body of technologies, processes, and practices designed to protect networks, computers, programs, and data from attack, damage, or unauthorized access. It refers to techniques and practices designed to protect digital data — stored, transmitted, or used on an information system. In simpler terms, cyber security is the protection of Internet-connected systems, including hardware, software, and data from cyber attacks.

The term "Cyber Security" is made up of two words:

- **Cyber** — related to technology which contains systems, networks, programs, or data.
- **Security** — related to protection which includes systems security, network security, and application and information security.

### Why is Cyber Security Important?

1. **Financial Impact** — Cyber attacks can be extremely expensive for businesses to endure. The cost includes direct losses, remediation costs, and regulatory fines.
2. **Reputational Damage** — In addition to financial damage, a data breach can inflict untold reputational damage to an organization.
3. **Evolving Threats** — Cyber attacks these days are becoming progressively destructive. Cybercriminals are using more sophisticated ways to initiate cyber attacks.
4. **Regulatory Compliance** — Regulations such as GDPR are forcing organizations into taking better care of the personal data they hold.

### Security Domains

Cyber security can be divided into several common categories:

1. **Network Security** — The practice of securing a computer network from intruders, whether targeted attackers or opportunistic malware.
2. **Application Security** — Focuses on keeping software and devices free of threats. A compromised application could provide access to the data it is designed to protect. Successful security begins in the design stage, well before a program or device is deployed.
3. **Information Security** — Protects the integrity and privacy of data, both in storage and in transit.

### Conclusion

Cyber security is essential in today's digital world as organizations depend heavily on digital infrastructure. A comprehensive security strategy must address all domains — network, application, and information security — to provide layered defense against modern threats.

## Q2. Explain the various types of Cyber Attacks in detail.

### Introduction

A cyber attack is an attempt by hackers to damage or destroy a computer network or system. Attackers have various motives including financial gain, recognition, revenge/fun, and political motivation. Cyber attacks can be categorized based on their nature and target.

### Categories of Cyber Attacks

**1. Attacks to Retrieve Personal Information:**

- **Phishing Attack** — Conducted through emails, SMS, or weblinks to trick users into revealing personal information.
- **Pharming Attack** — A social engineering cyberattack where criminals redirect internet users trying to reach a specific website to a different, fake site. Pharming is more effective than phishing because it doesn't require the user to click a link.

**2. Attacks for Leakage of Information:**

- **Skimming Attack** — Copying the cardholder's personal payment information using devices called skimmers, mostly inside ATM or POS terminals, to store victims' card numbers and PINs.
- **Side Channel Attacks** — An attack enabled by leakage of information from a physical cryptosystem.
- **Eavesdropping or Snooping** — Intercepting data over communication links.

**3. Intruding into a Computer System:**

- **Password Guessing Attacks** — A special case of dictionary attacks where attackers try common passwords.
- **Identity Theft** — The attacker impersonates the victim to gain unauthorized access.

**4. Interruption/Disruption of Computing Services:**

- **Denial of Service (DoS)** — Making computing power, memory, and bandwidth unavailable to legitimate users.
- **Slowing Down Web Server** — Overloading the server to degrade performance.
- **Website Defacement** — Modifying the content of government or corporate websites.

**5. Malware Attacks:**

- **Virus** — A program that damages a computer system and replicates itself; requires a host.
- **Worm** — A self-replicating program that can delete files or send documents via email; negatively impacts network traffic.
- **Trojan** — A malicious program disguised as legitimate software; cannot replicate.
- **Logic Bomb** — Malware that lies dormant until triggered by a specific event; does not self-replicate.
- **Spyware, Keylogger, Ransomware, Rootkit** — Used for monitoring, password capture, blocking access, and gaining unauthorized control.

### Conclusion

Understanding the different types of attacks is the first step in building a robust defense. Organizations must implement layered security mechanisms to protect against these varied threats.

## Q3. Explain the types of Vulnerabilities in Cyber Security.

### Introduction

A vulnerability is a weakness in a procedure, protocol, hardware, or software in an organization that has a potential to cause damage. Vulnerabilities provide the entry points that attackers exploit to launch cyber attacks.

### Types of Vulnerabilities

**1. Human Vulnerabilities:**

- Induced by human behaviour.
- Example: A user clicks on a malicious link or email message (phishing attack, cross-site scripting attack).
- Human error is considered one of the most common and dangerous vulnerabilities because it can bypass even the strongest technical defenses.

**2. Protocol Vulnerabilities:**

- Attacks on network protocols such as TCP, IP, ARP, etc.
- Example: Attack on ARP protocol to sniff passwords from the LAN (Pharming Attack, Hijacking Attacks, Man-in-the-Middle Attack).
- These exploit weaknesses in the fundamental communication protocols that were not designed with security in mind.

**3. Software Vulnerabilities:**

- Related to application software.
- **Buffer Overflow** — Executing malicious worm code in a program.
- **Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)** — Web server related vulnerability that allows injection of malicious scripts.
- **SQL Injection** — Inserting malicious SQL queries to manipulate databases.
- These arise from coding errors and insecure development practices.

**4. Configuration Vulnerabilities:**

- Related to configuration settings on newly installed applications, files, etc.
- Example: **Privilege Escalation Attack** — An attacker gains elevated access due to misconfigured permissions.
- Default configurations often leave systems open to attack.

### Conclusion

Identifying and mitigating vulnerabilities is a continuous process. Organizations must perform regular vulnerability assessments, patch management, and security audits to minimize their attack surface. A combination of technical controls, proper configuration, and user awareness training is essential for comprehensive protection.

## Q4. Explain the Defense Strategies and Techniques in Cyber Security.

### Introduction

Defense strategies and techniques are the countermeasures employed to protect systems, networks, and data from cyber attacks. These strategies provide a layered approach to security, ensuring that even if one layer is breached, other layers continue to provide protection.

### Defense Strategies

**1. Access Control — Authentication and Authorization:**

- Access control is a method of limiting access to a system or to physical/virtual resources.
- Access control systems perform **identification**, **authentication**, and **authorization** of users.
- **Authentication** (Proof of Identity): Verifying who the user is using:
  - Password/PIN
  - Biometric (fingerprint, face recognition)
  - Card/Key (smart card, token)
- **Authorization**: After successful authentication, the system determines if the client has permission to use a resource or access a file. It is a process by which a server determines if the client has permission to use a resource.

**2. Data Protection:**

- Means assuring **Data Confidentiality** and **Data Integrity**.
- Cryptographic techniques are used for encryption and decryption.
- Methods include integrity check techniques, cryptographic checksum, and symmetric/asymmetric encryption algorithms.

**3. Prevention and Detection:**

- Access control and message encryption are **preventive strategies**.
- **Code Testing** is used to detect vulnerabilities (black box testing, white box testing).
- **Intrusion Prevention Techniques** — Checks for worm signature, pattern, or behaviour.
- **Packet Monitoring** — Continuous monitoring of network packets for anomalous behaviour.

**4. Response, Recovery, and Forensics:**

- Once an attack or infection is detected, **response measures** need to be taken, such as shutting down the system.
- During worm detection, infected parts of the system need to be **quarantined** and required patches must be applied.
- **Cyber Forensics** — A process of extracting data as proof for a crime. It involves collecting, preserving, and analyzing digital evidence.

### Conclusion

A comprehensive defense strategy requires implementing all four layers — access control, data protection, prevention/detection, and response/recovery. This defense-in-depth approach ensures robust protection against both known and emerging cyber threats.

## Q5. Explain Modular Arithmetic and its properties with examples. Discuss its relevance in Cryptography.

### Introduction

Modular arithmetic is a fundamental mathematical concept used extensively in cryptography. It deals with the remainder obtained when one integer is divided by another. It forms the mathematical backbone of many encryption algorithms.

### Definition

Let d be an integer and let n be a positive integer. Let q and r be the quotient and remainder obtained from dividing d by n. The relationship is:

**d = n × q + r, where 0 ≤ r < n**

We say "d is equal to r modulo n" if the remainder obtained from dividing d by n is r. This is expressed as:

**r ≡ d (mod n)**

### Example

Let n = 10 and r = 3. Then 13, 23, 33, etc. all satisfy the equation with quotients 1, 2, 3, etc. The set {..., -37, -27, -17, -7, 3, 13, 23, 33, 43, ...} all satisfy 3 ≡ d (mod 10).

### Properties of Modular Arithmetic

1. **Addition Property:**
   [(a mod n) + (b mod n)] mod n = (a + b) mod n

2. **Subtraction Property:**
   [(a mod n) - (b mod n)] mod n = (a - b) mod n

3. **Multiplication Property:**
   [(a mod n) × (b mod n)] mod n = (a × b) mod n

### Relevance in Cryptography

In cryptography, we often have to perform computations such as multiplying a large number of terms, each term itself being a very large number. For example, we may have to multiply 50 integers, each about 1000 digits long. Modular arithmetic properties allow us to:

- Keep intermediate results small by applying the modulo operation at each step.
- Perform efficient computation on very large numbers.
- Form the basis of algorithms like RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and others.

### Conclusion

Modular arithmetic provides the essential mathematical framework for modern cryptographic algorithms. Its properties enable efficient computation with large numbers, making secure encryption and decryption practical.

## Q6. Explain the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) and Euclid's Algorithm with an example.

### Introduction

The Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) is a fundamental concept in number theory and plays an important role in cryptography, particularly in key generation algorithms like RSA.

### Definition

The greatest common divisor (GCD) refers to the greatest positive integer that is a common divisor for a given set of positive integers. For a set of positive integers (a, b), the GCD is the greatest positive number which is a common factor of both a and b.

**Key Properties:**

- GCD of any two numbers is never negative or 0, as the least positive integer common to any two numbers is always 1.
- If gcd(b, c) = 1, we say that b and c are **relative prime** or **co-prime**.
- An integer is prime if it is co-prime with all positive integers less than it.

### Methods to Find GCD

1. By finding the common divisors
2. By Euclid's Algorithm (more efficient for large numbers)

### Euclid's Algorithm

The GCD of two positive integers (a, b) can be calculated as:

- If a = 0, then GCD(a, b) = b
- If b = 0, then GCD(a, b) = a
- If both a ≠ 0 and b ≠ 0, write 'a' in quotient remainder form: a = b × q + r (where a > b)
- Find GCD(b, r) as GCD(b, r) = GCD(a, b)
- Repeat until the remainder is 0.

### Solved Example

**Find GCD of 12 and 10:**

- a = 12, b = 10 → 12 = 10 × 1 + 2 → GCD(12, 10) = GCD(10, 2)
- a = 10, b = 2 → 10 = 2 × 5 + 0 → GCD(10, 2) = GCD(2, 0)
- a = 2, b = 0 → GCD(2, 0) = 2
- **Therefore, GCD(12, 10) = 2**

### Conclusion

Euclid's algorithm is an efficient method for finding the GCD of large numbers, which is critical in cryptographic key generation. It breaks down large numbers into smaller numbers iteratively until the GCD is found.

## Q7. Explain the Algebraic Structures — Groups, Rings, and Fields — used in Cryptography.

### Introduction

Groups, Rings, and Fields are fundamental elements of abstract algebra (modern algebra). These algebraic structures are widely used in cryptographic algorithms to define operations on keys and data.

### 1. Group

A group (G) is indicated by {G, _}. It is a set of elements with a binary operation '_' that satisfies four properties:

- **Closure** — If a and b are elements of G, then c = a \* b is also an element of G.
- **Associativity** — If a, b, and c are elements of G, then (a _ b) _ c = a _ (b _ c).
- **Identity** — For all a in G, there exists an element e in G such that e _ a = a _ e = a.
- **Inverse** — For each a in G, there exists an element a' (inverse of a) such that a _ a' = a' _ a = e.

An **Abelian Group** satisfies an additional property:

- **Commutativity** — For all a and b in G, a _ b = b _ a.

### 2. Ring

A ring R is indicated by {R, +, ×}. It is a set of elements with two binary operations (addition and multiplication) satisfying:

- R is an **abelian group** regarding addition (properties A1 through A5).
- **(M1) Closure under multiplication** — If a and b belong to R, then ab is also in R.
- **(M2) Associativity of multiplication** — a(bc) = (ab)c.
- **(M3) Distributive Laws** — a(b+c) = ab + ac and (a+b)c = ac + bc.
- **(M4) Commutativity of multiplication** — ab = ba.
- **(M5) Multiplicative identity** — There exists 1 in R such that a·1 = 1·a = a.
- **(M6) No zero divisors** — If a, b in R and ab = 0, then a = 0 or b = 0.

### 3. Field

A field F is indicated by {F, +, ×}. It is a set of elements that:

- Satisfies all axioms of a Ring (A1 through A5 and M1 through M6) — making it an integral domain.
- **(M7) Multiplicative Inverse** — For each a in F (except 0), there is an element a⁻¹ in F such that a·a⁻¹ = a⁻¹·a = 1.

### Conclusion

These algebraic structures provide the mathematical foundation for cryptographic algorithms. Fields, in particular, are used in AES and elliptic curve cryptography, while groups form the basis of Diffie-Hellman key exchange.

## Q8. Compare Secret Key (Symmetric) and Public Key (Asymmetric) Cryptography. Also explain the types of attacks on cryptographic algorithms.

### Introduction

Cryptography provides message confidentiality, integrity, authentication, and digital signatures. It uses plaintext, ciphertext, encryption, decryption, and keys. **Kerckhoff's Principle** states that the secrecy should be in the key used for decryption, not in the encryption or decryption algorithm.

### Secret Key vs Public Key Cryptography

| Parameter           | Symmetric (Secret Key)                                          | Asymmetric (Public Key)                                   |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Key**             | Uses a single shared (secret) key for encryption and decryption | Uses two different keys for encryption and decryption     |
| **Ciphertext Size** | Same or smaller than plaintext                                  | Same or larger than plaintext                             |
| **Efficiency**      | Efficient, recommended for large amounts of text                | Inefficient, used only for short messages                 |
| **Speed**           | Faster (single key)                                             | Slower (two keys with complex mathematical process)       |
| **Purpose**         | Mainly for bulk data transmission                               | For smaller transactions and establishing secure channels |
| **Security**        | Less secure (single key)                                        | Safer (two keys)                                          |
| **Algorithms**      | 3DES, AES, DES, RC4                                             | RSA, DSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECC, ElGamal                    |

### Types of Attacks on Cryptographic Algorithms

A cryptographic algorithm is considered secure if a cryptanalyst is unable to:

- (a) Obtain the corresponding plaintext from a given ciphertext
- (b) Deduce the secret key or private key

**Attack Types:**

1. **Known Ciphertext Attack** — The attacker only has access to the ciphertext and tries to determine the plaintext or key.
2. **Known Plaintext Attack** — The attacker has access to both plaintext and its corresponding ciphertext.
3. **Chosen Plaintext Attack** — The attacker can choose arbitrary plaintext and obtain its corresponding ciphertext.
4. **Brute Force Attack** — The attacker tries every possible key until the correct one is found.

### Conclusion

The choice between symmetric and asymmetric cryptography depends on the use case. In practice, hybrid systems use asymmetric cryptography for key exchange and symmetric cryptography for bulk data encryption, combining the strengths of both.

## Q9. Explain the DES (Data Encryption Standard) construction, its round structure, and modes of operation.

### Introduction

DES (Data Encryption Standard) is a symmetric key encryption algorithm that uses a Feistel structure. It is one of the most studied and influential encryption algorithms and forms the basis for understanding modern block ciphers.

### DES Construction

DES uses a **Feistel structure** consisting of:

1. **Initial Permutation** — The 64-bit plaintext block undergoes an initial permutation.
2. **16 Rounds** of a given function — The core encryption process.
3. **32-bit Left Right Swap** — After 16 rounds, the left and right halves are swapped.
4. **Final Permutation** — The inverse of the initial permutation produces the ciphertext.

### Product Ciphers

DES is a product cipher that uses:

- **P-box (Permutation box)** — Rearranges bits.
- **S-box (Substitution box)** — Substitutes bits according to a predefined table.

### DES Round Structure

DES uses two 32-bit halves (L and R). For any Feistel cipher:

- **Li = Ri-1**
- **Ri = Li-1 ⊕ F(Ri-1, Ki)**

The function F takes a 32-bit R half and a 48-bit subkey:

1. **Expansion** — Expands R from 32 bits to 48 bits using permutation E.
2. **XOR** — Adds the 48-bit subkey using XOR operation.
3. **S-box Substitution** — Passes through 8 S-boxes to get a 32-bit result.
4. **Permutation** — Finally permutes using a 32-bit permutation P.

### Modes of Operation

DES supports four modes of operation (also applicable to AES):

1. **ECB (Electronic Code Book)** — Each block encrypted independently. Simple but reveals patterns.
2. **CBC (Cipher Block Chaining)** — Each block XORed with previous ciphertext block before encryption.
3. **CFB (Cipher Feedback)** — Converts block cipher to stream cipher for real-time processing.
4. **OFB (Output Feedback)** — Similar to CFB but feedback is from the output of the encryption function.

### Conclusion

DES, despite being superseded by AES for modern applications, remains fundamental in understanding symmetric encryption. Its Feistel structure, round function, and modes of operation are concepts used in many modern algorithms.

## Q10. Explain Elementary Substitution and Transposition Ciphers. Also compare Block Cipher and Stream Cipher.

### Introduction

Substitution and transposition ciphers are the two fundamental techniques of classical cryptography. Substitution replaces characters, while transposition rearranges them. Modern ciphers can be categorized into block ciphers and stream ciphers.

### Elementary Substitution Ciphers

**1. Caesar Cipher:**

- Each letter in the plaintext is substituted for another unique letter by shifting a fixed number of positions in the alphabet.
- Simple but easily broken.

**2. Monoalphabetic Cipher:**

- The cipher line can be any permutation of the 26 alphabetic characters.
- A single cipher alphabet (mapping from plain alphabet to cipher alphabet) is used per message.
- Vulnerable to frequency analysis since English language nature of plaintext is known.

**3. Polyalphabetic Cipher:**

- The ciphertext corresponding to a particular character in the plaintext is not fixed.
- Uses multiple substitution alphabets.
- Examples: **Vigenere Cipher** and **Hill Cipher**.

### Elementary Transposition Ciphers

- Transposition means shuffling; it rearranges or permutes the bits in a block of plaintext.
- The characters remain the same but their positions change.

### Block Cipher vs Stream Cipher

| Parameter           | Block Cipher                                         | Stream Cipher                                   |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Definition**      | Converts plaintext by taking each block individually | Converts plaintext by taking one byte at a time |
| **Principle**       | Uses both diffusion and confusion                    | Uses only confusion principle                   |
| **Decryption**      | More difficult (more bits combined)                  | Easier (XOR can be quickly reversed)            |
| **Implementation**  | Feistel Cipher is the most popular                   | Vernam Cipher is the main implementation        |
| **Reversibility**   | Difficult to reverse                                 | Easy to reverse (XOR)                           |
| **Algorithm Modes** | ECB, CBC                                             | CFB, OFB                                        |
| **Complexity**      | Simple design                                        | Complex comparatively                           |
| **Bits Used**       | 64 bits or more                                      | 8 bits                                          |

### Cipher Properties — Confusion and Diffusion

- **Confusion** — Ensures that the ciphertext has no information about the plaintext.
- **Diffusion** — Output bits must be challengingly dependent on input bits, so that changing one bit of plaintext changes many bits of ciphertext.

### Conclusion

Classical ciphers laid the foundation for modern cryptography. Understanding substitution, transposition, and the differences between block and stream ciphers is essential for grasping how modern encryption algorithms work.

**1. Define Cyber Security.**
Cyber security is the body of technologies, processes, and practices designed to protect networks, computers, programs, and data from attack, damage, or unauthorized access. It is the protection of Internet-connected systems, including hardware, software, and data from cyber attacks.

---

**2. What is a Cyber Attack?**
A cyber attack is an attempt by hackers to damage or destroy a computer network or system. The main motives behind cyber attacks include financial gain, recognition, revenge/fun, and political motivation.

---

**3. Define Phishing Attack.**
Phishing is a type of cyber attack conducted through emails, SMS, or weblinks to trick users into revealing personal information. Attackers disguise themselves as trustworthy entities to steal sensitive data like passwords and credit card numbers.

---

**4. What is a Pharming Attack?**
Pharming is a type of social engineering cyberattack in which criminals redirect internet users trying to reach a specific website to a different, fake site. It is more effective than phishing because it doesn't require the user to click a link.

---

**5. Define Virus and Worm.**

- **Virus**: A program that attempts to damage a computer system and replicates itself to other systems. It requires a host to replicate.
- **Worm**: A self-replicating program that can delete files or send documents via email. Unlike a virus, it does not need a host.

---

**6. What is a Trojan Horse?**
A Trojan horse is a malicious program that is disguised (masquerades) as legitimate software. The compromise of a user account could lead to the compromise of the entire environment. Unlike viruses and worms, it cannot replicate itself.

---

**7. What is Ransomware?**
Ransomware is a type of malicious software designed to block access to a computer system until a sum of money is paid. It encrypts the victim's files and demands a ransom for the decryption key.

---

**8. Define Vulnerability in Cyber Security.**
A vulnerability is a weakness in a procedure, protocol, hardware, or software in an organization that has a potential to cause damage. Types include human, protocol, software, and configuration vulnerabilities.

---

**9. What is Denial of Service (DoS) Attack?**
Denial of Service (DoS) is a cyber attack that makes computing power, memory, and bandwidth unavailable to legitimate users. It aims to disrupt services by overwhelming the target system with excessive requests or traffic.

---

**10. Define Authentication and Authorization.**

- **Authentication**: Proof of identity — verifying who the user is using password/PIN, biometrics, or card/key.
- **Authorization**: A process by which a server determines if the client has permission to use a resource or access a file after successful authentication.

---

**11. What is Cyber Forensics?**
Cyber forensics is a process of extracting data as proof for a crime. It involves collecting, preserving, and analyzing digital evidence from computer systems, networks, and storage devices to be used in legal proceedings.

---

**12. Define Modular Arithmetic.**
In modular arithmetic, if d = n × q + r (where 0 ≤ r < n), we say "d is equal to r modulo n." It is expressed as r ≡ d (mod n). Modular arithmetic is fundamental to cryptographic algorithms like RSA and Diffie-Hellman.

---

**13. What is GCD? State Euclid's Algorithm.**
GCD (Greatest Common Divisor) is the greatest positive integer that is a common divisor for a given set of positive integers. Euclid's Algorithm: If a = b × q + r, then GCD(a, b) = GCD(b, r). Repeat until remainder is 0.

---

**14. What are Co-prime Numbers?**
If gcd(b, c) = 1, we say that b and c are relative prime or co-prime. This means they share no common factor other than 1. An integer is prime if it is co-prime with all positive integers less than it.

---

**15. Define Group in Algebra.**
A group {G, _} is a set of elements with a binary operation '_' that satisfies four properties: Closure, Associativity, Identity, and Inverse. If it also satisfies commutativity, it is called an Abelian group.

---

**16. Define Ring and Field.**

- **Ring** {R, +, ×}: A set with two operations (addition and multiplication) where R is an abelian group under addition and satisfies closure, associativity, and distributive laws for multiplication.
- **Field** {F, +, ×}: A ring that additionally has a multiplicative inverse for every non-zero element.

---

**17. State Kerckhoff's Principle.**
Kerckhoff's Principle states that the secrecy of a cryptographic system should be in the key used for decryption, not in the encryption or decryption algorithm. The algorithm can be publicly known; only the key must remain secret.

---

**18. Define Confusion and Diffusion in Cryptography.**

- **Confusion**: Ensures that the ciphertext has no information about the plaintext, making it hard to find the key.
- **Diffusion**: Output bits must be dependent on input bits such that changing one bit of plaintext changes many bits of ciphertext.

---

**19. What is a Caesar Cipher?**
Caesar Cipher is an elementary substitution cipher where each letter in the plaintext is substituted for another unique letter by shifting it a fixed number of positions in the alphabet. For example, with a shift of 3, A becomes D.

---

**20. What is Linear Cryptanalysis?**
Linear cryptanalysis is a known plaintext attack in which the attacker studies probabilistic linear relations (linear approximations) among parity bits of the plaintext, the ciphertext, and the hidden key. It involves identifying linear relationships and using known plaintext-ciphertext pairs.

---

**21. What is a Logic Bomb?**
A Logic Bomb is malware that lies dormant until triggered by a specific event. The trigger may be a specific date and time, the launching of a program, or processing of a specific activity. It does not self-replicate.

---

**22. What is a Skimming Attack?**
Skimming is an act of copying the cardholder's personal payment information. Criminals install a small electronic device called a skimmer, mostly inside ATM or POS terminals, to store hundreds of victims' card numbers and PINs.

---

**23. What is MAC (Message Authentication Code)?**
MAC (Message Authentication Code) is a cryptographic technique used to verify both the integrity and authenticity of a message. It produces a fixed-size output (tag) using a secret key shared between sender and receiver.

---

**24. List the Modes of Operation in DES.**
The four modes of operation used by DES (also applicable to AES) are:

1. ECB (Electronic Code Book)
2. CBC (Cipher Block Chaining)
3. CFB (Cipher Feedback)
4. OFB (Output Feedback)

---

**25. Differentiate between Block Cipher and Stream Cipher.**

- **Block Cipher**: Encrypts plaintext by taking each block individually (64 bits or more). Uses both confusion and diffusion. Example: Feistel Cipher.
- **Stream Cipher**: Encrypts plaintext one byte at a time (8 bits). Uses only confusion. Example: Vernam Cipher.
