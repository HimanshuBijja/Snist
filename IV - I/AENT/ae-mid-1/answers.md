### 1.3 Strategy

- **Part-A questions ask ONE thing each** — no double-barrelled questions in this paper. Two crisp points plus one example per answer is enough for full 2 marks.
- **Q5 is the only higher-order verb** ("Elucidate", L6). It wants *why crowd funding matters* — reach, no-debt/no-equity option, validation — not just the definition. Give the definition in one line, then 3 importance points.
- **Q10 is the safest 6 marks in Part-B.** Its three parts are pure recall lists (skills / secondary revenue / importance of a team) drawn from all three units, so partial knowledge still scores.
- **Q4 and Q10(b) are literally the same question.** Learn it once, use it twice.
- **Q7 is the highest-effort** (L4 "Illustrate … with suitable examples") — you *must* name real companies against each model; theory alone loses marks. But it is also the most predictable, since the source lists exactly 10 models with examples.
- **Recommended attempt:** Q10 + Q7 + (Q8 or Q9, whichever you know better). Q9 is a list-heavy L1 question and is easier than Q8 if you memorise the Equity/Debt split.

## 2. Solved Blueprint Paper

### PART-A (6 × 2 = 12 Marks)

**Q1. Define the concept of Pivoting. (Unit-I, L1, 2M)**

A **pivot** means **fundamentally changing the direction of a business** when the entrepreneur realises the current products or services are **not meeting the needs of the market**. Its main goal is to help the company **improve revenue or survive in the market**. Pivoting means creating a new product/service or modifying the existing one, and it answers the questions *"Does the customer need this product?"* and *"Is this the right customer segment?"* **Pivots are the essence of entrepreneurs and the key to start-up success.**
**Example:** **RedBus** pivoted from bus-booking software for bus operators to an online ticketing platform for customers.

**Q2. What are the functions of entrepreneurs? (Unit-I, L1, 2M)**

The **eight main functions** of an entrepreneur are:
**1. Planning** — new product development, procuring resources, distribution channel, customer segment, commercialization.
**2. Risk and uncertainty bearing** — modifying existing products/services to retain customers.
**3. Organization building** — assigning roles and responsibilities so the business goal is attained.
**4. Managing** — managing, collaborating and coordinating with all entities.
**5. Decision making** — deciding on a new market, a new product, or both (diversification).
**6. Innovation** — a continuous activity to remain competitive in the market place.
**7. Leading** — leading the team from idea generation to final sales/delivery.
**8. Managing growth** — improving market share and creating a differential advantage.

**Q3. What is financial planning? (Unit-II, L1, 2M)**

A **financial plan** is a **document containing a person's current money situation and long-term monetary goals, as well as the strategies to achieve those goals**. Financial planning is a **step-by-step approach to meet one's life goals** — it helps you be in control of your **income, expenses and investments** so that money is available **at the right point in time**.
Its features are **Foresight, Flexibility, Optimal usage of funds, Simplicity and Liquidity**.
**Example:** Building a corpus of Rs. 10 lakh for a daughter's college education by the time she turns 18 — not a year later.

**Q4. Write the secondary sources of revenue for a start-up. (Unit-II, L1, 2M)**

**Secondary revenue** refers to revenue generated from **goods or services which differ from the main product or service lines** of a company. It is also called **ancillary revenue, non-core revenue or monetisation**. (Primary revenue, by contrast, is the **sale of the company's own goods or services**.)
**Common secondary sources:** selling **advertising space**, **commission** from partners, **subscription / premium add-ons**, **data monetisation**, **training, consulting and after-sales service**, and **renting out idle assets or facilities**.
**Example:** **Vida e Caffe** does not only sell coffee — it sells **advertising space** in its quarterly magazine *Obrigado*, on its takeaway cup holders and on tags attached to its in-store mugs.

**Q5. Elucidate the importance of crowd funding. (Unit-III, L6, 2M)**

**Crowd funding** is the **accumulation of small investments in individual projects by a large number of individuals (the "crowd")** via the Internet and social networks — *"the collective effort of individuals who network and pool their money to support efforts initiated by other people or organizations."*
**Importance:**
1. It lets a start-up **raise a large amount from many small contributors** when banks and VCs are not accessible.
2. **Rewards-based and donation-based** crowd funding raise money **without debt and without giving away equity**.
3. It **validates the idea** — the public's willingness to fund proves market demand.
4. It provides **publicity and an early customer base** along with the money.
**Examples:** Kickstarter, Indiegogo, GoFundMe, Crowdcube, Quirky.

**Q6. Write the advantages of bootstrapping. (Unit-III, L1, 2M)**

**Bootstrapping** is the practice of **self-financing a business using only existing resources** — no venture capital and no major loans.
**Advantages:**
1. The **business will be worth more** because **less money has been borrowed** (no dilution of ownership).
2. **No high interest** has to be paid on borrowed money.
3. The entrepreneur becomes **creative in finding ways to raise profits** without having to look to external sources.
**Examples of bootstrapped enterprises:** Dell, Facebook, Apple, Coca-Cola, HP, Microsoft, Oracle, eBay, Cisco, SAP.

## 4. Part-A Bank (2-Mark Q&As)

### UNIT-I

**Q. Define entrepreneurship.**
Entrepreneurship is the **professional application of knowledge, skills and competencies to generate a new business idea**, by an individual or a set of people, by **launching an enterprise** to pursue growth while generating **wealth, employment and social good**. It is a combination of **passion, vision and action**.

**Q. Who is an entrepreneur?**
An entrepreneur is a person who **establishes and invests in a business, managing the company and the uncertainties that come with it**, and is likely the **primary financial beneficiary**. The entrepreneur is the **actor**, entrepreneurship is the **act**, and the outcome is the **enterprise**.

**Q. How did Peter Drucker define entrepreneurship?**
Drucker defined entrepreneurship as **"a systematic innovation, which consists in the purposeful and organized search for changes, and the systematic analysis of the opportunities such changes might offer for economic and social innovation."**

**Q. What is an enterprise?**
An enterprise is the **business organization that is formed and which provides goods and services**, creates jobs, and contributes to **national income, exports and overall economic development**.

**Q. Distinguish between entrepreneur, entrepreneurship and enterprise.**
The **entrepreneur is the actor** (the person who starts it), **entrepreneurship is the act** (the process of creation), and the **enterprise is the outcome** — the business organization that results.

**Q. Mention the qualities of entrepreneurship.**
**Six qualities:** (1) Ability to innovate, (2) Business-oriented tendency, (3) Organizing function, (4) Managerial and leadership function, (5) Knowledge-based function, (6) Based on **principles, not on intuition**.

**Q. Mention the different skills of entrepreneurship.**
**Communication skills, Human relations skills, Math skills, Problem-solving and decision-making skills, Technical skills, and Business skills.**

**Q. What are the three things an entrepreneur must build?**
1. **Build a business model** — the right business model (plan for making a profit); for every customer segment there should be one business model.
2. **Build a sustainable venture** — carry the same model forward with slight modifications in the Lean Canvas template.
3. **Build a right pitch** — identifying the right problem is the key to any business's success.

**Q. What is a business model?**
A business model is an **outline of how a company plans to make money with its product and customer base in a specific market**. At its core it explains four things: **what product/service will be sold, how it will be marketed, what expenses it will face, and how it expects to turn a profit**.

**Q. What does a business model emphasize?**
**Price of the product, revenues, cost of production, investment in sales/marketing/product development, and profit and cash flow.**

**Q. What is a value proposition?**
A **value proposition** is a **feature that makes your product attractive to your customers**. The **Unique Value Proposition (UVP)** is a precise statement reflecting the promise of value the product delivers — relevancy, quantified value and unique differentiation. **Example:** YouTube — *"Flickr for video"*.

**Q. What is competitive advantage?**
A **unique feature of your product or service that cannot easily be copied by competitors**.

**Q. What is an unfair advantage in the Lean Canvas?**
Something that **cannot easily be copied or bought by the competition** and is compelling enough for target customers to choose your product over existing alternatives — e.g. deep domain knowledge, intense focus (Google's focus on search), or a brand community (Facebook, Apple ecosystems).

**Q. What is a Lean Canvas?**
A Lean Canvas is an **entrepreneur-focused business model template designed for emerging businesses**. It takes only minutes to create and helps build the business faster by **capturing the idea, collecting feedback and iterating dynamically**.

**Q. What is cost structure in a business model?**
A **list of the fixed and variable expenses** your business requires to function, and **how they affect pricing**.

**Q. What are key metrics?**
**Key metrics are the ways your company measures success** — e.g. new user acquisition (app downloads, registrations), engagement (likes, reposts, referrals), retention (monthly active users) and expenses (ad costs, travel).

**Q. Define pivoting.**
A pivot means **fundamentally changing the direction of a business** when the current products/services aren't meeting market needs, in order to **improve revenue or survive**. **Ex:** RedBus.

**Q. State the need for pivoting.**
Don't build a product nobody wants (**Ex: Google Glass**); check whether the solution actually solves the customer's problem; check **problem–solution fit and product–market fit**; growth has hit a plateau; fall in returning customers; changing market needs. **Ex:** Minglebox, unable to compete with Facebook, introduced an online education portal.

**Q. When is it the right time to pivot a business?**
When (1) you see little progress despite huge money and resources, (2) there is too much competition, (3) progress has plateaued, (4) only one feature/service gets traction, (5) customers aren't responding as expected, (6) your perspective about the industry has changed. **A pivot should be the last resort.**

**Q. What is NPS?**
**NPS (Net Promoter Score)** tests **product–market fit** by asking *"How likely are you to recommend company X to a friend or colleague?"* The responses are converted into a quantifiable score: **NPS ≥ 0 is GOOD; NPS ≤ 0 is NEGATIVE and the business needs to be pivoted.**

**Q. Differentiate business plan and business model.**
A **business plan** is a **road map of how you develop a product or service** — a formal plan for achieving business goals. A **business model** answers **how you deliver value-added products/services and monetize that value**, laying out the problem, the solution and how the business generates revenue. **The business plan is created based on the business model.**

**Q. What is refinement of a business model?**
Refining the business model means **solving the same problem differently**. After refinement, the **cost structure should decrease, revenue streams should increase and new distribution channels get added**. **Refining a business model means pivoting.**

**Q. Why do we refine a business model?**
To **grow revenues**, come up with **fresh ideas and stay in competition**, become **more profitable**, address **changing customer tastes**, bring in **new technologies and trends**, **comply with changes in taxes and custom duty**, and address a **large customer database**.

**Q. Give examples of companies that refined their business model.**
**SLACK** — a failed 2008 online game refined into a professional chat service. **LEGO** — from a toy/building-block manufacturer to a model where customers select from 1000 different blocks, assemble a structure and order it. **LENSKART** — from an online e-tailer to brick-and-mortar eye stores, because customers wanted to select lenses personally and get computerized eye-power checks.

**Q. Why do we add a new customer segment?**
To **increase revenues and expand the business**. You must identify who your existing customers are, whether more segments can be added, and whether you are selling **B2B or B2C** (and whether more can be added in either). **Ex:** OLA served only B2C in 2010, added B2B corporate services in 2016 (OLA Corporate, LUX, Share). **Ex:** YouTube launched YouTube Kids in 2017.

**Q. What is product management?**
Product management is the function that **bridges the gap between the business and the customer** — presenting changing customer needs and trends, bridging product design and customer usage, collaborating engineers/designers/customers, and helping create blockbuster products. **Ex:** OLA — Cab aggregator, Share, Auto, Outstation, Prime, Credit.

**Q. Who is a product manager?**
The Product Manager is **responsible for delivering a differentiated product to market that addresses a market need and represents a viable business opportunity**, ensuring the product supports the company's **overall strategy and goals**. He/she is often called the **"CEO of the product."**

**Q. What does a product manager do?**
**Defines the product vision, strategy and roadmap; gathers, manages and prioritizes market/customer requirements; acts as the customer advocate; and works closely with engineering, sales, marketing and support** to meet the business case and customer satisfaction goals.

**Q. What are the key components of a product manager's role?**
**Domain expertise** (knowledge of the market and product area), **Business expertise** (keeping the product profitable), **Leadership skills** (guiding many people in the company), and **Operational ability** (handling nitty-gritty details such as part numbers and spreadsheets).

**Q. Give examples of product manager failures.**
**1. P&G Gillette Vector razor** — designed for Indian men after 3000 hours of work; it declogged under running water, but Indian men use a cup of water, so it failed. **2. Snapchat (Nov 2017)** — a UI redesign for "simple personalized navigation" led **1.2 million users to sign a rollback petition** and a sharp fall in stock price.

### UNIT-II

**Q. What is a business plan?**
A business plan is a **written document that describes in detail how a business — usually a start-up — defines its objectives and how it is to go about achieving its goals**. It lays out a written roadmap for **sales, financial and human resource planning**.

**Q. Why are business plans important?**
They are **used to attract investment before a company has established a proven track record**, they help companies **start, manage and grow**, they keep the company **on target going forward**, and they act as a means to **get people to work with and invest in the business**.

**Q. What are the types of business plans?**
Two categories: **Traditional** and **Lean Start-up**. **Lean start-up business plans use an abbreviated structure highlighting key elements** and can be **as short as one page**; they are less common in the business world. **There is no right or wrong business plan.**

**Q. What are the components of a business plan?**
**Five components:** (1) **Executive summary**, (2) **Business description and structure** (products and services), (3) **Market research and strategies** (market analysis + marketing strategy), (4) **Management and personnel**, (5) **Financial documents**.

**Q. What does the executive summary contain?**
It **outlines the company** and includes the **mission statement** along with information about the company's **leadership, employees, operations and location**.

**Q. What does the business description and structure section contain?**
The **products and services** offered, with **pricing, product lifespan and consumer benefits**; also **production and manufacturing processes, patents, proprietary technology and R&D information**.

**Q. What does market analysis cover in a business plan?**
The company's **competition and how the company fits in the industry**, its **relative strengths and weaknesses**, the **expected consumer demand**, and how easy or difficult it is to **grab market share from incumbents**.

**Q. What does the marketing strategy section describe?**
How the company will **attract and keep its customer base** and how it intends to **reach the consumer**; a **clear distribution channel** must be outlined, plus **advertising and marketing campaign plans** and the media those campaigns will use.

**Q. What is a sales plan?**
A sales plan defines **target customers, business objectives, tactics, obstacles and processes**, along with the **resources and strategies** used to achieve targets. It works like a business plan but **focuses only on the sales strategy**.

**Q. What should an effective sales plan do?**
**Communicate the company's goals and objectives to the sales team; provide strategic direction; outline roles and responsibilities for the team and leadership; and monitor the team's progress towards organizational goals.**

**Q. List the components of a sales plan.**
(1) **Company mission and positioning**, (2) **Sales organization structure**, (3) **Sales goals and targets (KPIs)**, (4) **Target audience and customer segments**, (5) **Sales strategies and methodologies**, (6) **Sales execution plan**, (7) **Support** (financial resources, tools, training).

**Q. What kind of sales team should companies hire?**
Companies need **multi-dimensional sales people** — those who **collaborate with others, help customers, use analytics and work social media**. Steps: make sure you're ready to hire commission-only reps, put your best foot forward, provide sales training, explain your sales process clearly, and create the right power dynamic.

**Q. What is Human Resource Planning (HRP)?**
HRP is the **continuous process of systematic planning ahead to achieve optimum use of an organization's most valuable asset — quality employees**. It ensures the **best fit between employees and jobs** while **avoiding manpower shortages or surpluses**.

**Q. What are the four steps of the HRP process?**
(1) **Analyzing present labor supply**, (2) **Forecasting labor demand**, (3) **Balancing projected labor demand with supply**, (4) **Supporting organizational goals**.

**Q. What is workforce/people planning?**
Identifying the **strengths and skills you will need people to have, the work they will need to do, and when you will need them**, in order to meet future objectives.

**Q. List the people-plan strategies for a start-up.**
(1) **Treat your applicants well**, (2) **Think about cost/benefit before you hire**, (3) **Try a freelancer**, (4) **Get potential employees with previous start-up experience**, (5) **Strengthen your employer brand**, (6) **Use an employee referral program**.

**Q. What is financial planning?**
A financial plan is a **document containing a person's current money situation and long-term monetary goals, as well as strategies to achieve those goals**. It is a **step-by-step approach to meet one's life goals**, controlling income, expenses and investments.

**Q. What are the features of financial planning?**
**Foresight** (estimating future risks and asset needs), **Flexibility** (allowing future adjustments), **Optimal usage of funds** (utilizing idle money and assets), **Simplicity** (understandable even to a layman), and **Liquidity** (keeping current assets as money for salaries, fees and other payments).

**Q. What is financial forecasting?**
Financial forecasting is the process of **predicting or estimating the future state of an organization** — how the business will perform in future — **based on historical data** such as the income statement, position statement, current conditions and past trends, usually undertaken to **prepare budgets and allocate available resources**.

**Q. What two things does financial forecasting estimate?**
**Revenue and expenses.**

**Q. State the roles of financial forecasting in business.**
(1) **New business promotion**, (2) **Seamless functioning** (anticipating future roadblocks), (3) **Estimating financial requirements** (sales, cost of customer acquisition, project capital), (4) **Control cash flow**, (5) **Achieve overall success** (foundation for organization-wide budgeting).

**Q. What is a procurement plan?**
A procurement (or procurement management) plan is a **document used to manage the process of finding and selecting a vendor**. It **justifies the need for an external supplier** and explains how the supplier will be found — from **identifying project requirements to closing the contract**.

**Q. What is the goal of a procurement plan?**
To **increase the efficiency, effectiveness and transparency of the procurement process**, and to help leaders decide **what to buy, when to buy it and what sources to purchase from**, avoiding surprises and last-minute considerations.

**Q. What does a procurement plan include?**
The **types of contracts** to be used, the **planned delivery/implementation dates**, the **metrics used to evaluate vendor performance**, and an **explanation of how the procurement process will be performed**.

**Q. Why is negotiation important, and what are the role-play guidelines?**
Negotiation **determines whether a customer purchases and at what price**, so negotiating skills are crucial for the sales team. **Guidelines:** write down one personal area for improvement; choose who plays the salesperson and who plays the prospect; go through a standard negotiation; debrief after reaching agreement (or deciding needs are incompatible); **switch roles**.

**Q. What is a revenue stream?**
A revenue stream is a **distinct source of income** that can be **recurring revenue, transaction-based, or service revenue**. A business can have a **single source or multiple sources** depending on its business model.

**Q. What are primary revenue sources?**
For any company, the primary revenue source is the **SALE of goods or services** — the company's own main product/service lines.

**Q. What is secondary revenue?**
Revenue generated from **goods or services which differ from the main product or service lines** of a company. Also called **ancillary revenue, non-core revenue, or monetisation**. **Ex:** Vida e Caffe selling advertising space.

**Q. What is the customer lifecycle?**
In customer relationship management, the customer lifecycle **describes the various stages a consumer goes through before, during and after completing a transaction** — the **Point A to Point B journey** until the final purchase.

**Q. Why is customer lifecycle management important?**
It **can make or break your ability to upsell, cross-sell and grow an existing customer relationship**, and it helps companies **cultivate brand loyalty** by identifying opportunities to add value at key points in time.

**Q. List the five stages of the customer lifecycle.**
**1. Reach/Awareness → 2. Acquisition → 3. Conversion → 4. Retention → 5. Loyalty.** (Also stated as **Reach, Acquire, Develop/Nurture, Retention, Advocacy**.) It is an **endless loop**, not a one-way path.

**Q. What happens in the acquisition stage?**
Shoppers **reach your platforms — website, social media, customer care — to know more about your product**, and this is where you **capture potential customers' contact details** (for e-commerce, getting shoppers to sign up for an account).

**Q. What is advocacy in the customer lifecycle?**
Once retention is reached, you want customers to become **brand advocates** — truly satisfied customers **recommend your products to friends and family**, spreading awareness in their social circles so their extended network is more likely to convert.

### UNIT-III

**Q. Who is an angel investor?**
An angel investor is a **wealthy individual who invests his or her own money in a company — usually a start-up in the early stages of development**, usually in exchange for **convertible debt or ownership equity**. Their capital is **unsecured — they have no claim on the company's assets**. The term was first used by **William Wetzel**.

**Q. What is seed capital?**
Seed capital is the **initial amount of money an entrepreneur uses to start a business**, often coming from **family, friends, early shareholders or angel investors**. It supports the **planning of the business up to the point when the company starts selling a product or service**.

**Q. What is venture capital?**
Venture capital is capital **used to support start-ups and other businesses with the potential for substantial and rapid growth**. **VC firms raise money from limited partners (LPs)** to invest in promising start-ups or larger venture funds, in exchange for an **ownership share**.

**Q. How does SEBI define a venture capital fund?**
SEBI (Regulation 1996) defined it as **a fund established in the form of a company or trust which raises money through loans, donations, or the issue of securities/units, and makes or proposes to make investments in accordance with the regulations.**

**Q. List the equity sources of funding.**
(a) **Personal sources**, (b) **Private borrowings**, (c) **Informal capital**, (d) **Corporate securities**, (e) **Public issues**, (f) **Internal financing**.

**Q. List the debt sources of funding.**
(a) **Debentures**, (b) **Commercial papers**, (c) **Inter-corporate deposits**, (d) **Public deposits**, (e) **Institutional sources**, (f) **Commercial banks**, (g) **Non-institutional arrangements (hire, rent and leasing)**.

**Q. Differentiate equity financing and debt financing.**
**Equity:** no repayment of invested funds; **higher risk** for the investor (no guarantee); **less repayment pressure** but pressure to hit growth targets; return is **capital growth**; investors **involve themselves in decision making**. **Debt:** funds repaid **within a stipulated time with interest**; **lower risk** (collateral); **more repayment pressure**; return is **interest payments**; lenders have **very little involvement**.

**Q. What is bootstrapping?**
Bootstrapping is the practice of **self-financing a business, using only existing resources** — no venture capital and no major loans. It is **one of the best and most inexpensive routes** for raising capital — *"a way to pull yourself up without the help of others."*

**Q. Write the advantages of bootstrapping.**
(1) The **business is worth more because less money has been borrowed**; (2) **no high interest** is paid on borrowed money; (3) the entrepreneur becomes **creative in finding ways to raise profits** without external sources.

**Q. Name some bootstrapped enterprises.**
**Dell Computers, Facebook, Apple, Clorox, Coca-Cola, Hewlett-Packard, Microsoft, Oracle, eBay, Cisco Systems and SAP.**

**Q. What are the three funding stages of bootstrapping?**
(1) **Beginning stage** — personal savings, or money borrowed/invested by friends and family, or a side income; (2) **Customer-funded stage** — money from customers keeps the business operating and funds growth; (3) **Credit stage** — funding specific activities such as improving equipment or hiring staff, through loans or even venture capital for expansion.

**Q. List the sources of bootstrap financing.**
**Trade credit, Factoring, Customers, Real estate, and Equipment suppliers** (via conditional sales contract and chattel mortgage contract).

**Q. What is trade credit?**
A supplier extends you credit — typically **30, 60 or 90 days without charging interest** — once you are a regular customer. **Ex:** a bill due in 30 days with net-60-day terms gives you **30 extra days** to pay.

**Q. What is factoring?**
Factoring is a financing method where you **sell your accounts receivable to a buyer, such as a commercial finance company, to raise capital**. The **"factor" buys the receivables at a discount rate of one to 15 percent**, becomes the creditor, and takes over collection and paperwork. It can be done on a **non-notification basis**, where your customers aren't aware their accounts were sold.

**Q. How can customers be a source of bootstrap financing?**
By having them **write you a letter of credit**. **Ex:** a large corporation orders cloth bags from your firm; you obtain a **letter of credit from the customer when the order is placed** and use it as **security to purchase the material** from your supplier.

**Q. How is real estate a source of bootstrap financing?**
By **leasing your facility rather than buying it** — this **reduces start-up costs** — and by **negotiating lease payments that correspond to seasonal peaks or growth patterns**.

**Q. What are the two credit contracts used to finance equipment purchases?**
(1) **Conditional sales contract** — the purchaser **does not receive title** to the equipment until it is fully paid for. (2) **Chattel-mortgage contract** — the equipment **becomes the purchaser's property on delivery**, but the seller **holds a mortgage claim** against it until the contract amount is paid.

**Q. Name the five types of angel investors.**
(1) **The Family Investor**, (2) **The Relationship Investor**, (3) **The Idea Investor**, (4) **The Once-Removed Investor**, (5) **The "Archangel" Investor**.

**Q. What is an Archangel investor?**
An **"Archangel"** is a Relationship Investor or Idea Investor who has a **track record of success in making other angels money**. They have **built a successful company in the same space** or have **strong personal contacts with other angel investors**.

**Q. Describe the investment profile of angel investors.**
Angel investments bear **extremely high risk** and so require a **very high return**. Professional angels seek investments that can **return at least 10 times the original investment within 5 years** through an IPO or acquisition; best practice suggests aiming for **20x–30x over five to seven years**. The **actual effective IRR of a typical successful portfolio is only about 20–30%**.

**Q. Name some top angel investors in India.**
**Rajan Anandan, Anupam Gopal Mittal, Sanjay Mehta, Zishaan Hayath, Sharad Sharma, Anand Ladsariya and Sunil Kalra.**

**Q. What are the characteristics of venture capitalists?**
**Well-developed managerial skills, well-knowledgeable, sophisticated investors who come forward to take risks, decision-making ability, and analyzing skills.**

**Q. What services do venture capitalists provide?**
(1) **Finance to new and rapidly growing firms**, (2) **finance to knowledge-based, sustainable and scalable companies**, (3) **purchase of equity and quasi-equity securities**, (4) **assistance in the development of new products/services**, (5) **adding value through active participation**.

**Q. Give the advantages and disadvantages of venture capital.**
**Advantages:** **economy-oriented, investor-oriented and entrepreneur-oriented.** **Disadvantages:** it is a **lengthy and complex process**, involves **dealing in negotiation**, and **investors become part-owners of the business**.

**Q. Differentiate venture capitalists and angel investors.**
A **VC is a company/business**, an **angel is an individual investor**. VCs are **seldom interested in early stage**; angels **invest in early-stage or start-up businesses**. VC investment is **more than £1 million**; angel investment is **£10k–£100k** (groups up to £1m). VCs **require a seat on the board**; angels may be **"hands-off" or "hands-on"**, adding important skills.

**Q. What is an incubator?**
An incubator is an **organization, platform or team of experienced professionals that helps start-ups bootstrap during their early stages**, providing **mentoring, guidance, co-working space and sometimes funding**. It is traditionally the **first port of call for a budding entrepreneur**.

**Q. State the advantages of incubators.**
(1) **Validation of idea** (market research, feedback, market-ready prototype), (2) **Mentoring support**, (3) **Co-working space** (a melting pot of ideas), (4) **Support in fund raising** (investors trust well-known incubators), (5) **Shared resource pool** of experts, including technology.

**Q. What is a startup accelerator?**
An accelerator is an organization that offers **mentorship, capital and connections to investors and business partners**, designed for **select start-ups with promising Minimum Viable Products (MVPs)** as a way to **rapidly scale growth**. Start-ups enter for a **fixed period (3–6 months) as part of a cohort**.

**Q. Differentiate incubators and accelerators.**
**Accelerators** are **funded by an existing company**, aim at **scaling companies up**, run for a **clearly delineated few months**, feature **mentoring by the legacy company**, and are **highly structured** — for a proven start-up needing a cash injection. **Incubators** are **often independent** (or tied to VC firms/universities), focus on **stimulating innovation**, are **longer term (even years) and open-ended**, are **less selective and larger in number**, and focus on **creating an environment for co-creation** — for earlier-stage companies and solo first-time founders.

**Q. Define crowd funding.**
The **accumulation of small investments in individual projects by a large number of individuals (the "crowd")** via or with the help of the Internet and social networks — *"the collective effort of individuals who network and pool their money to support efforts initiated by other people or organizations."*

**Q. Name the four types of crowd funding.**
**Rewards-based, Donation-based, Equity-based and Debt-based (peer-to-peer / P2P).**

**Q. What is rewards-based crowd funding?**
It attracts **patrons who invest because they enjoy your project and want to support it**. Popular sites: **Kickstarter and Indiegogo**. **Main advantage:** you **don't launch in debt** and **don't give out shares**.

**Q. What is equity-based crowd funding?**
Crowd funding **aimed at technological, industrial and professional businesses** rather than causes. Sites: **Crowdcube, Fundable, Seedrs, Fig**. **Main advantage:** you can **collect a larger amount of money** and launch the business **without taking credit**.

**Q. What is debt-based crowd funding?**
Also known as **peer-to-peer (P2P)** — like a bank credit, but instead of borrowing one large amount you **borrow smaller amounts from multiple people**. Platforms: **Funding Circle and Zopa**. **Main advantage:** **no bank loan application, paperwork or strict demonstrable credibility** required.

**Q. Name some crowd funding platforms and their specializations.**
**Crowdfunder** (business and tech start-ups), **CrowdRise** (charities and causes), **Quirky** (inventors), **Invested.in** (create your own funding community), **Indiegogo** (no specialization), **GoFundMe** (donation-based).

**Q. What are the types of commercial bank loans?**
**Asset-based:** accounts receivable loans, inventory loans, equipment loans, real-estate loans. **Cash-flow financing (conventional bank loans):** installment loans, straight commercial loans, long-term loans, character loans.

**Q. What is a funding plan?**
A funding plan is a **document that outlines how an idea is going to be financed by an entrepreneur**. It helps in **mapping out possible methods of income generation, identifying potential funders, and planning the development of funding applications**.

**Q. What are the components of a good funding plan?**
(1) **Identify the funding needs** — how much money and for what purpose; (2) **identify potential methods of income generation** — both self-generation and external sources; (3) **identify potential funders** and gather information on their application processes; (4) **plan out how and when you will apply for funds**.

**Q. What is a pitch deck?**
A pitch deck is a **presentation deck used to pitch an idea or company to an audience, generally investors**. It gives a **brief overview of the business plan, products, services and growth traction**, and must be **organized based on the audience and forum**. A good rule of thumb is **no more than 19 slides**.

**Q. What are the key components of a pitch deck?**
**High-level summary slides, the problem being addressed, the product, the market/strategy, the team, financials/projections, and the tone used to convey the idea.**

**Q. Write the importance of building a start-up team.**
About **60 percent of start-ups fail to take off due to poorly constituted teams**. Behind every successful start-up is a **team of passionate individuals working towards a common goal**, and **regardless of how good the idea is, the team lays the stepping stone to success**. *"No matter how brilliant your mind or strategy, if you're playing a solo game, you'll always lose out to a team."* — Reid Hoffman.

**Q. What key qualities should employees hired for a start-up have?**
**Flexibility, passion and trustworthiness.** They should be **flexible enough to take on diverse responsibilities until further expansion**, and should fit the **company culture**.

**Q. What is the "hire slow, fire fast" philosophy?**
**Hire slow** — take time to find the right person; the classic case study is **Airbnb, which took about five months to hire its first employee**. **Fire fast** — ensure **only fitting candidates remain in the team**.

**Q. Name the eight start-up roles to hire.**
(1) **CEO and COO**, (2) **Product manager**, (3) **CTO / VP of engineering hybrid**, (4) **CMO**, (5) **Sales manager**, (6) **CFO**, (7) **Business development manager**, (8) **Customer service representative**.

**Q. How do you pitch to attract talent to a start-up?**
**Build an attractive company** (appeal to top applicants, define and share company values, offer **competitive pay** — essential for attraction and even more for retention); **reach candidates** by attending conferences, talks and tech meet-ups and by encouraging employee referrals; treat **tech recruiters as product partners**; **integrate product and hiring roadmaps**; and **communicate inside and out**.

#### Q7-A. Illustrate the various types of business models with suitable examples. ★ (Blueprint Q7)

**Definition:** A **business model** is an outline of **how a company plans to make money with its product and customer base in a specific market**. At its core it explains four things: **what product/service will be sold, how it will be marketed, what expenses it faces, and how it turns a profit.** It is the **dot-connecting between the value of products/services delivered and the customers**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Problem → Customer Segment → Solution → Revenue Model → Profit`

**The ten types of business models:**

**1. Manufacturing model** — Sells a **finished product to distributors** and then to the final customers. *Ex: Samsung, Philips, Cross, Rotomatic.*

**2. Distributor model** — **Buys finished products from manufacturers and sells to the retailer**, who sells directly to the customer. *Ex: Car dealerships, food chains.*

**3. E-tailer / Online model** — Sells products **online without a brick-and-mortar store**. *Ex: Flipkart, Amazon, LINIO.*

**4. Aggregator / Influencer (commission) model** — **Brings multiple service providers under one brand** and offers them to customers, **earning money through commission**. *Ex: OYO Rooms, Uber.*

**5. Franchise model** — Can be a distributor or retailer; uses the **product or business model of the parent through partnership** and pays **royalty fees** for using the brand. *Ex: The Body Shop, McDonald's, KFC.*

**6. Bricks and clicks model** — Offers **both offline and online shopping** opportunities to customers. *Ex: Croma, DMart, JioMart, clothing businesses.*

**7. Subscription model** — Customers **pay a fixed subscription fee at regular intervals** (monthly, annually) to access a service; it can be customized per business. *Ex: Netflix, Amazon Prime, The Gift Box.*

**8. Freemium model** — The most common model on the internet; a combination of **"free" and "premium"** — a **basic product is provided free of charge**, but a **premium is charged for additional features, services or goods** that expand the functionality of the free version. *Ex: Zendesk, Trello, Gmail.*

**9. SaaS (Software as a Service) model** — A **software licensing and delivery model in which software is licensed on a subscription basis and is centrally hosted**; also called **"on-demand software"** or web-hosted software, and is **part of cloud computing**. *Ex: MS Office, Zendesk, Dassault, Ansys, CATIA.*

**10. Marketplace model** — A **platform where vendors and customers buy and sell products**. Vendors need not pay for marketing, and customers pay for the **trusted platform**. A pure e-commerce marketplace follows a **zero-inventory model** — the company acts as a **meeting ground for buyers and sellers without storing goods**, but offers shipping and payment assistance by tying up with logistics and financial partners. *Ex: eBay, Naaptol, ShopClues, Shutterstock.*

**Conclusion:** As **Steve Blank** said, *"The rapid shifts in the business model is what differentiates start-ups from an established company."* An entrepreneur must choose the model that fits the customer segment — **one business model for every customer segment**.

#### Q7-B. Explain the Lean Canvas and how to approach it.

**Definition:** A **Lean Canvas** is an **entrepreneur-focused business model template designed for emerging businesses**. It takes only minutes to create and lets you **capture the idea, collect feedback and iterate on it dynamically**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Problem → Customer Segment → Solution → UVP → Unfair Advantage → Channels → Revenue Streams / Cost Structure → Key Metrics`

**Approach:** First identify the **problem or opportunity**, then define the **customer segment**, which helps identify the **innovative product or service** that offers the **right solution for that targeted segment**.

**The eight steps of the Lean Canvas template:**
1. **Brainstorm the target customers' problem** — put yourself in the customers' shoes, capture their **3 key frustrations**, and find the **existing alternatives** they use.
2. **Analyze target customer segments** — remember the customer is not necessarily the user (*users of dog food are dogs, but dog owners buy it*); find the **early adopters**.
3. **Formulate the Unique Value Proposition (UVP)** — a precise statement of the promise of value: **relevancy, quantified value and unique differentiation**. *Ex: YouTube — "Flickr for video"; Aliens — "Jaws in space".*
4. **Unfair advantage** — something that **cannot easily be copied or bought** — deep domain knowledge, intense focus (Google's search focus), or a brand community (Facebook, Apple).
5. **Envision your solution** — the product vision and features; ideally done in a **team workshop**; build the **smallest solution customers are ready to pay for**.
6. **Think of promotion channels** — free (social media, SEO, blogging) and paid (Facebook/Google/LinkedIn ads, directory listings, trade fairs); **think of retention before referral**.
7. **Revenue streams and cost structure** — start with a **single, simple pricing plan** that can be tested and revised.
8. **Write down key metrics** — acquisition (downloads, registrations), engagement (likes, reposts, followers, referrals), retention (monthly active users) and expenses (ads, travel).

**Example:** The Lean Canvas of **Uber** — problem: unreliable taxi availability; segment: urban commuters; solution: app-based on-demand cabs; revenue: commission per ride.

#### Q7-C. Explain pivoting — its meaning, need and when a business should pivot. Give examples.

**Definition:** A **pivot** means **fundamentally changing the direction of a business** when the current products or services **aren't meeting the needs of the market**. Its main goal is to **improve revenue or survive in the market**. **Pivots are the essence of entrepreneurs and the key to start-up success — if you can't pivot, chances are you will fail quickly.**

**What pivoting does:** creates a **new product/service or modifies the existing one**, and answers *"Does the customer need this product?"* and *"Is this the right customer segment?"*

**DIAGRAM:** `Existing Product → No Product-Market Fit → PIVOT → New Direction → Revenue & Growth`

**Need for pivoting:**
1. **Don't build a product that nobody wants.** *Ex: Google Glass.*
2. Check whether **the solution is indeed solving the customer's problem**.
3. Check **problem–solution fit and product–market fit**.
4. **Growth has hit a plateau.**
5. **Fall in the number of returning customers.**
6. **Changing needs of the market.** *Ex: Minglebox, unable to compete with the giant leader Facebook, introduced an online education portal.*

**When to pivot — a pivot is not a magic pill; it should be the last resort when all other options are exhausted.** Pivot if: you see **little progress despite huge money and resources**; there is **too much competition**; **progress has plateaued**; **only one feature/service gets traction**; **customers aren't responding as expected**; or **your perspective about the industry has changed**.

**Measuring the need — NPS:** The **Net Promoter Score** tests product–market fit by asking *"How likely are you to recommend company X to a friend or colleague?"* — **NPS ≥ 0 is GOOD; NPS ≤ 0 is NEGATIVE and needs to be pivoted.**

**Example:** **RedBus** started as **bus-booking software for bus operators** and pivoted into an **online ticketing platform for customers** — becoming one of India's biggest travel platforms.

#### Q7-D. Explain the refinement of a business model with suitable examples.

**Definition:** **Refining a business model means solving the same problem differently.** Importantly, **refining a business model means pivoting** — creating a new product or service model is pivoting, and pivoting helps in **getting customers and generating revenues**. It is essential when an entrepreneur's experiment fails.

**Reasons for refining a business model:**
1. To **grow revenues**.
2. To come up with **fresh ideas and stay in the competition**.
3. To become **more profitable**.
4. To address **changing tastes of customers**.
5. To bring in **new technologies and trends**.
6. To **comply with changes in taxes and custom duty**.
7. To address the **large customer database**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Refined Business Model → Cost Structure ↓ + Revenue Stream ↑ + New Distribution Channels`

**Five fine-tuning questions to increase revenue:**
1. **Are there people who could use my goods but don't have access?** *Ex: MTN and Vodacom pioneered "pay-as-you-go" cell phones in South Africa, tapping much larger markets.*
2. **Are people not buying because of the price point?** *Ex: Kulula.com expanded airline travel by dropping domestic ticket prices; Virgin Active created premium classic clubs at a higher price point for exclusivity.*
3. **Is everything you do actually providing value — could you simplify without hurting perceived value?** *Ex: Outsurance found not everyone valued a broker, and created the direct model for selling insurance.*
4. **Are there untapped additional revenue streams?** *Ex: Vida e Caffe sells advertising space in its magazine Obrigado, on cup holders and mug tags.*
5. **Could you alter the timing of revenue collection?** Most smaller businesses benefit by **getting cash in earlier to speed up cash flow**.

**Case examples:**
- **SLACK** — a **failed online game (2008)** refined its business model into a **professional chat service**.
- **LEGO** — moved from **building-block toy manufacturing** to a model where **customers select from 1000 different blocks, assemble a structure and order it**.
- **LENSKART** — reinvented from **online e-tailer to brick-and-mortar eye stores**, because customers wanted to personally select lenses and frames and get computerized eye-power checks and optometrist advice. Costs increased, but **revenues increased more** because customer needs were met.
- **FabIndia / Marks & Spencer** — refined by **adding online sales as a new distribution channel**; costs rose (website, payment gateways, digital marketing) but revenues increased.

#### Q7-E. How does an entrepreneur analyze a competitor's business model and add a new customer segment?

**Part 1 — Analyzing the competitor's business model.** Entrepreneurs must **constantly study the environment in which they operate** and **understand competitors' business models thoroughly**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Research Competitor's Business Model → Fill all 9 blocks of the Lean Canvas → Analyze strengths & weaknesses → Build your own Business Model`

**Steps (illustrated with prescription eyewear):**
1. **Study the competitor's model** — brick-and-mortar stores, onsite optometrists, the customer segments served, whether prices are too high or low, estimated costs, hiring expense, setup and investment costs, how they advertise, marketing opportunities, distribution channels, number of branches.
2. **Sketch the competitor's Lean Canvas** based on their **strengths** — e.g. optometrist available all day, fast turnaround for prescription glasses.
3. **Work out their weaknesses** — e.g. **high price, high investment cost**.
4. **Learn from the strengths and benefit from the weaknesses** — borrow all the strengths, overcome the weaknesses, and **frame your own Lean Canvas with those modifications**.
5. **Result:** a new model with **reduced setup cost, lower price, and the competitor's fast turnaround time** — which proves to be the best business model.

**Part 2 — Adding a new customer segment.** A new segment is added **to increase revenues and expand the business**. Questions to ask: **Who are my existing customers? Can I add more segments? Am I selling B2B or B2C? Can I add more in B2B, B2C or both?**

**Examples:**
- **OLA** — in **2010** it served only **B2C** (cabs booked via app). In **2016** it started **corporate B2B services**, where employees avail cabs through a prepaid account, avoiding unnecessary reimbursement. It launched **OLA Corporate** (corporate employees), **OLA LUX** (luxury cars — Audi, Benz) and **OLA Share** (shared rides at economical prices).
- **YouTube** — launched **YouTube Kids in 2017**, catering only to the kids segment with parental controls.
- **Men's shaving accessories → women** — requires a **separate business model**: a different solution (different blades, nicer handle), a **new distribution channel** (beauty stores and e-commerce), and a **relook at the cost structure**.

#### Q7-F. Explain product management and the significance and role of the product manager.

**Definition:** **Product management bridges the gap between the business and the customer.** A product can be successful only if it **continuously adapts to changes or undergoes modification according to customer tastes and preferences**.
*Ex: Microsoft's Windows OS has been constantly upgraded from 1985 through Windows 10 in 2015. ID (iD Fresh Food) came up with ready-made batter for idli/dosa in 2005, then ready-to-fry parathas/rotis and specialized vada in special packing.*

**What effective product management does:**
1. **Presenting the changing needs and trends of the customer.**
2. **Bridging the gap between product design and customer usage.**
3. **Collaborating engineers, designers and customers.**
4. **Helping customers come up with blockbuster products.**
*Ex: Product management was very effective at OLA — OLA Cab aggregator, OLA Share, OLA Auto, OLA Outstation, OLA Prime, OLA Prime Play, OLA Credit.*

**Who is a product manager?** The Product Manager is **responsible for delivering a differentiated product to market that addresses a market need and represents a viable business opportunity**, ensuring the product **supports the company's overall strategy and goals**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Interior Team (Engineering, Design, Packaging) → PRODUCT MANAGER → User Interface (User experience, Data analysis, Customer behaviour, Track customer feedback)`

**Scope of the job — key components:**
- **Domain expertise** — knowledge of the market and product area; knowing the customers and the business is the main reason you were hired.
- **Business expertise** — the PM is called the **"CEO of the product"**; making sure the company generates a profit.
- **Leadership skills** — many people in the company look to you for guidance.
- **Operational ability** — diving into nitty-gritty details such as creating part numbers or updating spreadsheets.

**What the product manager does:** **defines the product vision, strategy and roadmap; gathers, manages and prioritizes market/customer requirements; acts as the customer advocate** articulating the user's/buyer's needs; and **works closely with engineering, sales, marketing and support** to meet the business case and customer satisfaction goals.

**What happens if the product manager fails to foresee:** the company **faces losses and loses customer trust and loyalty**.
- **P&G's Gillette Vector razor** — 3000 hours of work by MIT graduates produced a razor that clogged after a single use and declogged under **running water**; but Indian men use a **cup of water**, so the product was a big failure.
- **Snapchat (Nov 2017)** — a UI change intended to simplify navigation for new users led **1.2 million users to sign a rollback petition** and caused a **sharp decline in stock prices**.

**Conclusion:** The product manager must **not only deliver what the customer wants but what delights them**, must constantly practise **observation, empathy and working on customer feedback**, and must **make the product more aligned to the customer**.

#### Q8-A. Explain business planning and the types of business planning. ★ (Blueprint Q8)

**Definition:** A **business plan** is a **written document that describes in detail how a business — usually a start-up — defines its objectives and how it is to go about achieving its goals**. It **lays out a written roadmap for the firm** covering **sales, financial and human resource planning**.

**Importance:** Business plans are **important documents used to attract investment before a company has established a proven track record**. They also **keep companies on target going forward**, help them **identify objectives and remain on track**, help them **start, manage and grow**, and act as **a means to get people to work with and invest in the business**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Business Model → Business Plan → Sales Plan + People Plan + Financial Plan + Procurement Plan → Goals achieved`

**Types of business plans:** Although **there is no right or wrong business plan**, they fall into two categories:

**1. Traditional business plan** — the detailed, conventional plan containing all five standard components (executive summary, business description, market analysis, marketing strategy, financial planning). It is thorough and is what **investors and banks typically expect**.

**2. Lean start-up business plan** — uses an **abbreviated structure that highlights only key elements**. These are **as short as one page** and are therefore **not as common in the business world**. They suit fast-moving start-ups that need to **iterate quickly**.

**Components of a business plan (five):**
1. **Executive summary** — outlines the company and includes the **mission statement** with information about **leadership, employees, operations and location**.
2. **Business description and structure (products and services)** — the products/services offered, with **pricing, product lifespan and consumer benefits**, plus **production and manufacturing processes, patents, proprietary technology and R&D**.
3. **Market research and strategies** — **Market analysis:** the competition, how the company fits in the industry, its relative strengths and weaknesses, expected consumer demand and how hard it is to grab market share from incumbents. **Marketing strategy:** how the company will **attract and keep customers**, the **distribution channel**, and the **advertising and marketing campaigns** and media used.
4. **Management and personnel** — the team and human resource requirements.
5. **Financial documents / financial planning** — the company's **financial planning and projections**; **financial statements and balance sheets** for established businesses, and **targets, estimates for the first few years plus a description of potential investors** for new businesses.

**Business Plan vs Business Model:** the **business plan is the road map of how you develop a product/service** and the formal plan for achieving business goals; the **business model answers how you deliver value-added products/services and monetize that value**, laying out the problem, the solution and how revenue and profits are generated. **Based on the business model, we create the business plan.**

#### Q8-B. Explain the sales plan and its components. How should a sales team be hired?

**Definition:** A **sales plan** defines the **target customers, business objectives, tactics, obstacles and processes**, along with the **resources and strategies used to achieve target goals**. It often includes **revenue goals and team structure**. It works similarly to a business plan in the way it is presented, but **focuses only on the sales strategy**.

**What an effective sales plan should do:**
1. **Communicate the company's goals and objectives to the sales team.**
2. **Provide strategic direction for the sales team.**
3. **Outline roles and responsibilities for the sales team and leadership.**
4. **Monitor the sales team's progress towards organizational goals.**

**DIAGRAM:** `Mission & Positioning → Org Structure → Goals & KPIs → Target Audience → Strategy → Execution → Support`

**Components of a sales plan (seven):**
1. **Company mission and positioning** — start broadly with the company's big-picture mission, then add positioning in the marketplace and the **unique selling proposition (USP)**.
2. **Sales organization structure** — an **organization chart of the sales team** outlining responsibilities and goals per role.
3. **Sales goals and targets** — high-level goals, **sales targets and KPIs** with a timeline; goals must be **realistic, ambitious and measurable**.
4. **Target audience and customer segments** — define **customer personas** upfront; enterprise sales teams may include **specific target accounts**.
5. **Sales strategies and methodologies** — the overall methodology, combining prior sections into a coherent strategy: **account-based sales (ABS), inbound sales and outbound sales strategies**.
6. **Sales execution plan** — explicitly **how you will operationalize the strategy** towards the goals.
7. **Support** — the **financial resources, tools, training and more** the team needs.

**Hiring the sales team:** Companies need sales teams that are **multi-dimensional — sales people who collaborate with others, help customers, use analytics and work social media**. Steps: **make sure you're actually ready to hire commission-only sales reps; put your best foot forward; provide the sales training your reps and teams need; explain your sales process clearly; and create the right power dynamic.**

#### Q8-C. Explain the People Plan / Human Resource Planning (HRP) for a start-up.

**Definition:** **Human resource planning (HRP)** is the **continuous process of systematic planning ahead to achieve optimum use of an organization's most valuable asset — quality employees**. It **ensures the best fit between employees and jobs while avoiding manpower shortages or surpluses**. HRP is an **important investment for any business** as it allows companies to remain **productive and profitable**.

**Workforce/people planning** means **identifying the strengths and skills you will need people to have, the work they will need to do, and when you will need them**, to meet future objectives.

**DIAGRAM:** `Analyze present labor supply → Forecast labor demand → Balance demand with supply → Support organizational goals`

**The four key steps of the HRP process:**
1. **Analyzing present labor supply** — what skills and people you already have.
2. **Forecasting labor demand** — what you will need in future.
3. **Balancing projected labor demand with supply** — closing the gap by hiring, training or redeploying.
4. **Supporting organizational goals** — aligning the workforce with business objectives.

**Six people-plan strategies for a start-up:**
1. **Treat your applicants well** — candidate experience shapes your brand.
2. **Think about the cost/benefit before you hire** — every hire is expensive for a start-up.
3. **Try a freelancer** — flexible capacity without a permanent commitment.
4. **Get potential employees with previous start-up experience** — they adapt to ambiguity.
5. **Strengthen your employer brand** — makes you attractive to talent.
6. **Use an employee referral program** — existing staff bring in trusted candidates.

**Example:** **Airbnb** took about **five months to hire its first employee** — the classic "hire slow" case, showing how carefully start-ups must plan people.

#### Q8-D. Explain financial planning and financial forecasting.

**Part 1 — Financial planning.** A **financial plan** is a **document containing a person's current money situation and long-term monetary goals, as well as strategies to achieve those goals**. It may be created independently or with the help of a **certified financial planner**, and in either case begins with a **thorough evaluation of the current financial state and future expectations**. Financial planning is a **step-by-step approach to meet one's life goals** — it helps you control **income, expenses and investments** so you can **manage your money and achieve your goals**. The connecting factor in every goal is **money — and you need it at the right point in time**.
*Example: to build a corpus of **Rs. 10 lakh for a daughter's college education**, the investment must grow to that amount **by the time she turns 18 — not a year later**.*

**Features of financial planning (five):**
1. **Foresight** — a plan without foresight results in disaster; foresight is needed to **estimate risks and the need for liquid and other assets**. It may not be 100% accurate but should estimate future risks.
2. **Flexibility** — the plan should allow **future adjustments according to needs**.
3. **Optimal usage of funds** — should **utilize idle money and assets** so they prove fruitful (excluding funds set aside for unforeseen circumstances).
4. **Simplicity** — simple in structure, providing a **sound allocation of resources easily understood even by a layman**.
5. **Liquidity** — keeping **current assets in the form of money**, helping easy allocation and payment of salary, fees and other kinds.

**Part 2 — Financial forecasting.** Financial forecasting is the process of **predicting or estimating the future state of an organization** — how the business will perform in future — **based on historical data** such as the **income statement, position statement, current conditions, past financial trends and the future internal and external environment**, usually undertaken to **prepare and develop budgets and allocate available resources for best possible utilization**. Its purpose is to **estimate two things: revenue and expenses**.

**Role of financial forecasting in business (five):**
1. **New business promotion** — helps utilize funds to promote new ventures and determine their success rate.
2. **Seamless functioning** — accurate forecasting of current revenue, revenue potential and expenses keeps the organization running smoothly and helps **anticipate future roadblocks**.
3. **Estimating financial requirements** — determines **sales, cost of customer acquisition, capital for a specific project** and other expenses, enabling sound decisions.
4. **Control cash flow** — organizations with good cash/bank balance are **more financially organized** and control operations better.
5. **Achieve overall success** — forms a **strong foundation for the complete budgeting of departments** across the organization.

#### Q8-E. Explain the customer lifecycle and its stages, and how it helps growth.

**Definition:** In customer relationship management, the **customer lifecycle describes the various stages a consumer goes through before, during and after they complete a transaction** — the **Point A to Point B journey** a customer takes until the final purchase.

**Importance:** Lifecycle management is a practice that can **make or break your ability to upsell, cross-sell and otherwise grow an existing customer relationship**, and it helps companies **cultivate brand loyalty by identifying opportunities for adding value at key points in time**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Reach/Awareness → Acquisition → Conversion → Retention → Loyalty ↺ (endless loop)`

**The five stages:**
1. **Reach / Awareness** — the first stage: shoppers **become aware of a problem or need** they must fulfil with a product. *Ex: we buy a light bulb when the old one goes off.* Your marketing material and content must be **where consumers will find it**.
2. **Acquisition** — shoppers **reach your platforms (website, social media, customer care)** to know more; this is where you **capture contact details** and, for e-commerce, get shoppers to **sign up for a customer account**. Reaching customers means little without **relevant content or messaging**; **personalized communication improves the odds of conversion**.
3. **Conversion** — when the customer **makes a purchase** (note that purchases can also happen during acquisition).
4. **Retention** — the **activities and actions you take to ensure customers keep coming back and keep buying**. It begins with **satisfying needs, caring for them and cultivating the relationship**; using **customer feedback analysis** to improve products makes customers feel part of the process and builds trust.
5. **Loyalty / Advocacy** — the last stage, but **not the end** — the lifecycle is an **endless loop** requiring continuous nurturing. Truly satisfied customers become **brand advocates**, recommending products to friends and family, and their **extended network is more likely to convert**.

*(A stage sometimes listed between acquisition and retention is **Develop/Nurture** — keeping in contact after the first purchase, ensuring satisfaction, using back-end analytics to predict what else they may like, and asking for feedback.)*

#### Q8-F. Explain the procurement plan and the importance of negotiation for a start-up.

**Part 1 — Procurement plan.** A **procurement plan** — also called a **procurement management plan** — is a **document used to manage the process of finding and selecting a vendor**. It **justifies the need for an external supplier** and explains **how the process of finding a supplier will be performed — from identifying the project requirements to closing the contract**.

**Goal:** to **increase the efficiency, effectiveness and transparency of the procurement process**. The document specifically describes **how products or services will be acquired and how vendors will be managed during the project**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Identify requirements → Find suppliers → Select vendor → Contract → Manage vendor → Close contract`

**Contents of a procurement plan:**
1. The **types of contracts** that will be used.
2. The **planned delivery or implementation dates** for the contracted products/services.
3. The **types of metrics used to evaluate the vendor's performance**.
4. An **explanation of how the procurement process will be performed**.

**Benefits:** it helps an organization **avoid surprises and last-minute considerations**, helps leaders decide **what to buy, when to buy it and what sources to purchase from**, and allows planners to determine whether **expectations and requirements are realistic** and achievable in the proposed project timeline.

**Part 2 — Negotiating role play.** **Negotiation plays an important role in determining whether a customer purchases, and if so, at what price** — a retailer may adopt negotiation. **Negotiating skills for sales team people are crucial**, and improving them **sets the foundation for a successful sale transaction**.

**Role-play guidelines:**
1. **Write down one personal area for improvement** related to negotiating.
2. **Choose who will play the salesperson and who will play the prospect.**
3. **Go through a standard negotiation.**
4. **Debrief** after you come to an agreement or decide your needs are incompatible.
5. **Switch roles.**

### UNIT-III

#### Q9-A. Write the various forms of funding options for an entrepreneur. ★ (Blueprint Q9)

**Definition:** Funding is the **capital an entrepreneur raises to start and grow a venture**. Broadly, all funding options fall into **two categories — Equity sources and Debt sources.**

**DIAGRAM:** `Funding Options → [ EQUITY: Personal, Private borrowings, Informal capital, Corporate securities, Public issues, Internal financing ] + [ DEBT: Debentures, Commercial papers, Inter-corporate deposits, Public deposits, Institutional sources, Commercial banks, Non-institutional (hire, rent, leasing) ]`

**1. Equity sources (six):**
(a) **Personal sources** — the entrepreneur's own savings.
(b) **Private borrowings** — from friends, family and known individuals.
(c) **Informal capital** — angel investors and other informal investors.
(d) **Corporate securities** — shares issued to corporate investors.
(e) **Public issues** — raising equity from the public through an IPO.
(f) **Internal financing** — ploughing back retained profits.

**2. Debt sources (seven):**
(a) **Debentures**, (b) **Commercial papers**, (c) **Inter-corporate deposits**, (d) **Public deposits**, (e) **Institutional sources**, (f) **Commercial banks**, (g) **Non-institutional arrangements — hire, rent and leasing**.

**3. The specific funding instruments an entrepreneur can use:**
- **Bootstrapping** — **self-financing using only existing resources**, no VC and no major loans. *Ex: Dell, Apple, Microsoft, eBay.*
- **Seed capital** — the **initial money used to start a business**, from family, friends, early shareholders or angels, supporting planning until the company starts selling.
- **Angel investors** — **wealthy individuals investing their own money** in early-stage start-ups in exchange for **convertible debt or ownership equity**; their capital is **unsecured**.
- **Venture capital** — supports **start-ups with potential for substantial and rapid growth**; VC firms raise money from **limited partners (LPs)** and take an **ownership share** and usually a **board seat**.
- **Incubators** — provide **mentoring, guidance, co-working space and sometimes funding** to very early-stage start-ups.
- **Accelerators** — provide **mentorship, capital and investor connections** to selected start-ups with promising MVPs, over a **fixed 3–6 month cohort**.
- **Crowd funding** — **accumulating small investments from a large crowd via the Internet**; four types: **rewards, donation, equity and debt (P2P)**.
- **Commercial bank loans** — **asset-based** (accounts receivable, inventory, equipment, real-estate loans) and **cash-flow financing** (installment, straight commercial, long-term and character loans).
- **Government loan schemes** — *Ex: Mudra Loan.*

**Equity vs Debt — how to choose:** with **equity** there is **no repayment of invested funds**, risk for the investor is **higher** (no guarantee), pressure is to **achieve growth targets**, the return is **capital growth**, and investors **involve themselves in decision making**. With **debt**, funds must be **repaid in a stipulated time frame with interest**, investor risk is **lower** (collateral), there is **more pressure to generate cash flows for interest repayment**, the return is **interest payments**, and lenders have **very little involvement**.

**Conclusion:** An entrepreneur typically moves through the funding ladder — **bootstrapping → friends and family/seed → angel → incubator/accelerator → venture capital → public issue** — choosing at each stage between **giving up ownership (equity)** and **taking on repayment obligations (debt)**.

#### Q9-B. Explain bootstrapping, its advantages, stages and sources.

**Definition:** **Bootstrapping is the practice of self-financing a business.** Using **only existing resources** — translation: **no venture capital or major loans** — bootstrapped companies **build their businesses from scratch**. Bootstrap financing is **probably one of the best and most inexpensive routes an entrepreneur can explore when raising capital** — it is *"a way to pull yourself up without the help of others."*

**Advantages:**
1. The **business will be worth more because less money has been borrowed.**
2. You **need not pay the high interest on borrowed money.**
3. You become **creative in finding ways to raise profits, without having to look to external sources.**

**Three funding stages of bootstrapping:**
1. **Beginning stage** — starts with **personal savings**, or **borrowed/invested money from friends and family**, or income from a side job.
2. **Customer-funded stage** — **money from customers keeps the business operating** and eventually **funds growth**.
3. **Credit stage** — the entrepreneur focuses on funding **specific activities such as improving equipment or hiring staff**; here the company **takes out loans or may even find venture capital for expansion**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Sources of Bootstrap Financing → Trade Credit | Factoring | Customers | Real Estate | Equipment Suppliers (Conditional Sales Contract / Chattel Mortgage Contract)`

**Sources of bootstrap financing (five):**
1. **Trade credit** — a supplier extends credit for **30, 60 or 90 days without charging interest** once you are a regular customer. *Ex: a bill due in 30 days with net-60 terms gives you 30 extra days to pay.*
2. **Factoring** — **selling your accounts receivable** to a buyer such as a commercial finance company to raise capital. The **factor buys receivables at a discount of 1–15 percent**, becomes the creditor and takes over collection and paperwork. On a **non-notification basis**, customers aren't even aware their accounts were sold.
3. **Customers** — having them **write you a letter of credit**. *Ex: a large corporation orders cloth bags; you take a letter of credit from the customer at order time and use it as security to buy material from your supplier in India.*
4. **Real estate** — **lease your facility rather than buy it**, which **reduces start-up costs**, and negotiate **lease payments matching seasonal peaks or growth patterns**.
5. **Equipment suppliers** — buy equipment **with a loan from the manufacturer** instead of paying cash, using either a **conditional sales contract** (purchaser gets title only when fully paid) or a **chattel-mortgage contract** (equipment becomes the purchaser's on delivery, but the seller holds a mortgage claim until paid).

**Examples of bootstrapped enterprises:** Dell Computers, Facebook, Apple, Clorox, Coca-Cola, Hewlett-Packard, Microsoft, Oracle, eBay, Cisco Systems, SAP.

#### Q9-C. Explain angel investors and venture capitalists, and differentiate them.

**Angel investor — definition:** A **wealthy individual willing to invest his or her own money in a business start-up**, usually in exchange for **convertible debt or ownership equity**. Also known as a **business angel, informal investor, angel funder, private investor or seed investor**. The term was **first used by William Wetzel**. Their capital is **unsecured — they have no claim on the company's assets**.

**Characteristics of angel investing:** usually a **high-net-worth individual funding start-ups at the early stages with their own money**; often the **primary source of funding for start-ups**, who find it more appealing than other, more predatory forms; the support **fosters innovation which translates into economic growth**; and these investments are **risky**.

**Five types of angel investors:**
1. **The Family Investor** — not a classic angel but a **supportive family member**, motivated by interest in supporting a family member or friend.
2. **The Relationship Investor** — a **co-worker from a previous company or a business friend**; supportive but also looking for a return.
3. **The Idea Investor** — invests **based on the idea with little emotional aspect**; often makes a good **early board member**.
4. **The Once-Removed Investor** — connected through a **personal or professional relationship** with either the Relationship or the Idea investor.
5. **The "Archangel" Investor** — a Relationship or Idea investor with a **track record of making other angels money**, who has **built a successful company in the same space** or has **strong contacts with other angels**.

**Investment profile:** angel investments bear **extremely high risk** and require a **very high return**. Since a large percentage are **lost completely when early-stage companies fail**, professional angels seek investments that can return **at least 10x or more within 5 years** via IPO or acquisition; best practice suggests aiming for **20x–30x over five to seven years**. The **actual effective IRR of a typical successful angel portfolio is only about 20–30%**.

**Venture capitalists — definition:** VCs **provide capital to young businesses in exchange for an ownership share of the business**. **SEBI (Regulation 1996)** defined a venture capital fund as **a fund established in the form of a company or trust which raises money through loans, donations or the issue of securities/units, and makes or proposes to make investments in accordance with the regulations.**

**Characteristics of VCs:** **well-developed managerial skills, well-knowledgeable, sophisticated investors who come forward to take risks, decision-making ability, and analyzing skills.**

**Services provided by VCs:** (1) **finance to new and rapidly growing firms**, (2) **finance to knowledge-based sustainable and scalable companies**, (3) **purchase equity and quasi-equity securities**, (4) **assist in the development of new products or services**, (5) **add value to the company through active participation**.

**Advantages of VCs:** **economy-oriented, investor-oriented, entrepreneur-oriented.** **Disadvantages:** **lengthy and complex process, deal in the negotiation, and investors become part-owners of the business.**

**Difference — VC vs Angel:**

| | Venture Capitalist | Angel Investor |
|---|---|---|
| Who | A **company or business** | An **individual investor** |
| Stage | **Seldom interested in early-stage** unless compelling (e.g. high tech with already successful founders) | **Willing to invest in early-stage/start-up** as well as established companies |
| Amount | **More than £1 million** | **£10k–£100k**, sometimes more; groups up to £1m |
| Maturity | Invests in **more mature and larger corporations** already established and seeking growth funding | Invests in **smaller start-ups with promising future outcomes** |
| Control | **Requires a seat on the board** | May be **"hands-off" or "hands-on"**, adding important skills |

#### Q9-D. Explain incubators and accelerators and differentiate between them.

**Incubators — definition:** An incubator is an **organization, platform or team of experienced professionals that helps start-ups bootstrap during their early stages**, often providing **mentoring, guidance, co-working space and at times some funding**. Traditionally, incubators are **the first port of call for any budding entrepreneur**. Starting up needs **active guidance, mentoring and a considerable amount of hand-holding**, which an incubator provides. **For an early-stage start-up still in its bootstrapping phase, incubators make perfect sense.**

**Advantages of incubators (five):**
1. **Validation of idea** — helps decide if the idea addresses market needs, carries out **market research**, gets **feedback**, and develops a **market-ready prototype**.
2. **Mentoring support** — a mentor with start-up experience or business acumen is a **good sounding board** and helps iron out issues.
3. **Co-working space** — a **vibrant melting pot of ideas** where entrepreneurs support each other through difficult times.
4. **Support in fund raising** — investors **trust start-ups from well-known incubators**, expecting the ideas to have been vetted.
5. **Shared resource pool** — access to **experts in specific areas, including technology**, for faster realization of the idea.

**Accelerators — definition:** A **startup accelerator** is an organization that offers **mentorship, capital and connections to investors and business partners**, designed for **select start-ups with promising Minimum Viable Products (MVPs)** as a way to **rapidly scale growth**. Start-ups enter for a **fixed period as part of a cohort**. The experience is **intense, rapid and immersive education**, compressing **years' worth of learning-by-doing into just a few months**. Accelerators are right for **start-ups ready to scale**, take **3–6 months** to get an early-stage start-up market-ready, and focus predominantly on **helping teams in the validation stage**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Idea → INCUBATOR (long term, guidance, co-creation) → MVP → ACCELERATOR (3–6 months, cohort, scale-up) → Growth`

**Differences:**

| S.No | Accelerators | Incubators |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Funded by an existing company** | **Often independent**, but may have connections to VC firms or universities |
| 2 | Aimed at **accelerating companies and scaling them up** | Focus primarily on **stimulating innovation** (incubating disruptive ideas) |
| 3 | A **clearly delineated time frame of a few months** | **Longer term — even years** — and more **open-ended** |
| 4 | **Mentoring by the legacy company** is a distinctive part of the program | Focus on **larger numbers** and are **less selective** |
| 5 | A **much more structured program**, creating alignment between the start-ups | More focused on **creating an environment for co-creation** |
| 6 | For a **proven start-up needing a cash injection to fuel growth** | For **earlier-stage companies or solo first-time founders needing guidance** |

#### Q9-E. Explain crowd funding and its types with examples.

**Definition:** **Crowd funding** is the **accumulation of small investments in individual projects by a large number of individuals (the "crowd") via or with the help of the Internet and social networks** — *"the collective effort of individuals who network and pool their money, usually via the Internet, to support efforts initiated by other people or organizations."* Most crowd funding platforms **specialize in a specific area such as art, business or charities**.

**DIAGRAM:** `Project Idea → Online Platform → Many small contributors (the Crowd) → Pooled Funds → Project launched`

**Types of crowd funding (four):**

**1. Rewards-based** — attracts **patrons who invest because they enjoy your project or initiative and want to support it**. The most popular reward-based sites are **Kickstarter and Indiegogo**. **Main advantage:** you **won't launch your venture in debt** (unlike debt-based) and **won't give out shares** (unlike equity-based).

**2. Donation-based** — typical for a **non-profit or a cause**. *Ex: GoFundMe.* **Main advantage:** as with rewards-based, **you don't need to take a loan to launch your project**.

**3. Equity-based** — aimed at **technological, industrial and professional businesses and services** rather than causes. *Ex: Crowdcube, Fundable, Seedrs, Fig.* **Main advantage:** you can **collect a larger amount of money** to launch the business **without taking credit**.

**4. Debt-based (peer-to-peer / P2P)** — like a bank credit, but instead of borrowing one larger amount, you **borrow smaller amounts from multiple people**. *Ex: Funding Circle, Zopa.* **Main advantage:** **no loan application, no bank paperwork, and no need for the strict demonstrable credibility a bank requires.**

**Platforms and their specializations:** **Crowdfunder** (business and tech start-ups), **CrowdRise** (charities and causes), **Quirky** (inventors), **Invested.in** (create your own funding community), **Indiegogo** (no specialization).

**Importance:** crowd funding gives access to capital **without debt or equity dilution** (for rewards/donation types), **validates market demand**, and **builds publicity and an early customer base** alongside the money.

#### Q9-F. Explain how to build an A-Team for a start-up, and the importance of doing so.

**Importance of building a start-up team:** Studies say that **about 60 percent of start-ups fail to take off due to poorly constituted teams.** This establishes that **behind every successful start-up is a team of passionate individuals who work tirelessly towards a common goal**, and that **regardless of how awesome the idea may be, building a team lays down the stepping stone to success.**
*"No matter how brilliant your mind or strategy, if you're playing a solo game, you'll always lose out to a team."* — **Reid Hoffman**
*"Great things in business are never done by one person."* — **Steve Jobs**
*"Teamwork makes the dream work, but a vision becomes a nightmare when the leader has a big dream and a bad team."* — **John Maxwell**

**DIAGRAM:** `Identify doers → Hire slow / Fire fast → Diverse team → Strong leadership → Scalable A-Team`

**How to build an A-Team for a start-up (nine points):**
1. **Identify the doers from the sayers** — have members who are **agile, resourceful and quick**, rather than those who merely spout off facts.
2. **Standard metrics don't apply** — candidates who are **passionate and self-driven** can be far more valuable than someone who appears competent but is ineffective.
3. **Adapt, change and improvise** — change the team based on the **gaps and future requirements**.
4. **Focus on customer service** — prefer a team that **makes the customer feel like a priority**.
5. **Avoid homogeneity** — a **heterogeneous work atmosphere** provides room for **creativity and individualistic ideas**.
6. **Exercise strong leadership** — the entrepreneur is **responsible for ensuring collaboration** so the outcome is as expected.
7. **Hire slow, fire fast** — the classic case is **Airbnb, which took about five months to hire its first employee**; "fire fast" ensures **only fitting candidates remain**.
8. **Experience vs raw talent** — both matter, but recruiters should look for **passion**; the **zeal for getting things done can never be compensated by experience or untapped potential**.
9. **Build a scalable team** — focus on building a **team of leaders** who can head their respective organisational units in the future.

**Hiring the A-Team members:** the people hired depend on the **specific business needs**; employees should have **flexibility, passion and trustworthiness**, and be **flexible enough to take on diverse responsibilities until further expansion**. When interviewing, **pose questions that probe drive and culture fit**, and use **hands-on simulations** to see how the candidate acts in common work situations. It is **smart to hire from the top, then expand when resources allow**, always keeping **company culture** in mind.

**Eight start-up roles to hire:** (1) **CEO and COO** — the CEO is the **big-picture person controlling direction, vision and culture**, while the COO focuses on **day-to-day operations**; (2) **Product manager** — manages **product strategy, vision and development**, working with engineering and marketing; (3) **CTO / VP of engineering hybrid** — crucial for tech start-ups; (4) **CMO** — focuses on **customers and how they view the product**; (5) **Sales manager** — **generates new leads and brings in money**; (6) **CFO** — responsible for **money and all aspects of company finances** (though experts recommend start-ups outsource accounting); (7) **Business development manager** — **grows the business from both marketing and sales standpoints**, developing partnerships with other businesses; (8) **Customer service representative** — **builds positive relationships with customers**, the cornerstone of the brand.

### 7.3 Commonly Confused Pairs

- **Pivoting vs Refining** — refining the business model **means** pivoting; refinement is solving the same problem differently, pivoting is changing direction. Treat them as the same idea in this syllabus.
- **Primary vs Secondary revenue** — primary is the **sale of the main goods/services**; secondary is from **goods/services different from the main line** (ancillary/non-core/monetisation).
- **Freemium vs Subscription** — Freemium gives a **basic version free** and charges for **extra features** (Gmail, Trello); Subscription charges a **fixed fee at regular intervals** for access (Netflix, Prime).
- **Aggregator vs Marketplace** — Aggregators bring providers **under one brand** and earn **commission** (OYO, Uber); Marketplaces are a **neutral platform** with **zero inventory** where vendors and customers transact (eBay, ShopClues).
- **E-tailer vs Bricks-and-clicks** — E-tailer is **online only, no physical store** (Amazon, Flipkart); Bricks-and-clicks is **both offline and online** (DMart, Croma, JioMart).
- **Conditional sales contract vs Chattel mortgage** — in a **conditional sale** you get **title only when fully paid**; in a **chattel mortgage** you get **ownership on delivery** but the seller **holds a mortgage claim**.
- **Angel vs Seed vs VC** — Angel = a **person's own money**, early stage; Seed = the **initial amount to start**, often family/friends/angels; VC = a **firm's LP money**, for rapid-growth companies.
- **Financial planning vs Financial forecasting** — Planning sets **goals and strategies to reach them**; Forecasting **predicts future revenue and expenses from historical data** to build the budget.
- **Business plan vs Funding plan** — the business plan is the **overall roadmap**; the funding plan specifically **outlines how the idea will be financed**.
- **Incubator vs Accelerator** — incubate = **create the idea** (long, open-ended); accelerate = **scale the MVP** (short, structured cohort).

### 7.4 Exam-Hall Tactics

1. **Part-A: two crisp points + one example per answer.** Don't write paragraphs for 2 marks — a definition line and a 2–3 point list scores full.
2. **Always name the company example.** RedBus (pivot), Google Glass (don't build what nobody wants), SLACK/LEGO/LENSKART (refinement), OLA/YouTube Kids (new segment), Vida e Caffe (secondary revenue), Airbnb (hire slow), Kickstarter/GoFundMe/Crowdcube/Zopa (crowd funding types).
3. **For Q7, tabulate.** Ten models × (definition + example) fits neatly as a two-column table and reads as complete coverage — that is exactly what an L4 "Illustrate with suitable examples" question rewards.
4. **Attempt Q10 first in Part-B** — it is three 2-mark lists and takes the least time, banking 6 marks before you tackle a long answer.
5. **Underline or bold keywords** — examiners scan for *pivot, product-market fit, Lean Canvas, ancillary revenue, bootstrapping, equity vs debt, MVP, cohort, 60 percent*.
6. **Draw the flow arrows.** Even a simple `Reach → Acquisition → Conversion → Retention → Loyalty` line earns diagram credit in a 6-mark answer.
7. **If short on time on Q9**, write the **Equity (6) / Debt (7) split** first — it is the literal answer to "various forms of funding options" — then add bootstrapping, angels, VC, incubators, accelerators and crowd funding as expansion.
8. **Watch the verbs:** "Define" → one-line definition; "Mention/Write" → a list; "Elucidate" → definition **plus** why it matters; "Explain" → definition + points; "Illustrate" → points + **examples and a diagram**.
